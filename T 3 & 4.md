# T3

# **Task 3: UI/UX and Frontend : Tool Form Exploration**

Owner: Aaina

Objective: Explore the different forms the instructor assistance tool could take and weigh the pros and cons of each, grounded in the accessibility needs of CNIB's five blind and low-vision instructors.

---

## **Context**

The tool needs to serve instructors who are all blind or low-vision and rely on assistive technology daily. "Accessible" here does not mean WCAG checkbox compliance. It means genuinely usable without a mouse, navigable by screen reader or keyboard alone, and low-friction enough that instructors adopt it without disrupting their existing teaching routines. Three form factors are worth evaluating.

---

## **Option A: Voice-Based Application**

A voice-first tool where instructors interact via spoken commands and receive audio feedback. Could be implemented as a standalone app, a Zoom-integrated audio layer, or a voice agent similar in spirit to CNIB's upcoming Clarity chatbot.

Pros:

* Directly matches how blind instructors already operate; screen readers and voice interfaces are daily tools, not novelties  
* Hands-free interaction means instructors can query data mid-class without switching contexts (e.g., "How many students submitted this week's workbook?")  
* Lower cognitive load during teaching; no visual scanning required  
* Natural fit for structured verbal prompts during class breaks  
* Aligns with the project's emphasis on non-invasive, passive data collection

Cons:

* Misrecognitions during a live class are disruptive and potentially embarrassing  
* Complex data like comparing 10 students' completion rates is difficult to convey through audio alone  
* Development complexity is higher: requires speech recognition, TTS pipeline, and intent parsing  
* Instructor privacy concerns if voice queries are audible to students during class  
* Harder to verify whether data returned is correct without a visual reference

Best for: Lightweight status checks and quick summaries during or between class sessions.

---

## **Option B: Screen-Reader-Optimized Visual Dashboard**

A structured web dashboard that presents student metrics, completion rates, attendance, and survey trends. Fully navigable by screen reader (NVDA, JAWS, VoiceOver).

Pros:

* Richer data presentation; tables, trends, and comparisons are easier to structure and then make accessible  
* Can integrate directly with Salesforce via API, keeping data in one place  
* Easier for sighted collaborators to co-use and review  
* More familiar paradigm for pre-session prep and longitudinal cohort review  
* Faster to prototype and iterate on

Cons:

* Screen-reader-accessible does not mean screen-reader-optimized by default; requires significant ARIA labeling, logical heading hierarchy, and keyboard navigation work  
* Not usable mid-class without disrupting teaching flow  
* Low-vision instructors may need high-contrast mode, large text, and specific color combinations that a generic dashboard will not provide without intentional design  
* Risk of information overload if too many metrics are surfaced at once

Best for: Pre-session prep, post-session debriefs, and longitudinal trend review across cohorts.

---

## **Option C: Hybrid Approach (Recommended)**

A screen-reader-optimized dashboard as the primary interface, with a lightweight voice command layer for mid-session use. Instructors use the dashboard before and after class, and trigger voice prompts during breaks.

Pros:

* Serves both use cases without compromise  
* Dashboard handles rich data; voice handles lightweight in-context queries  
* Allows phased development: ship the dashboard in iteration 1, add voice in iteration 2  
* Reduces risk; if voice recognition is imperfect, the dashboard provides full functionality  
* Mirrors how power AT users already work; many blind users combine screen reader, keyboard shortcuts, and voice for speed and accuracy

Cons:

* Highest total development scope of the three options  
* Two surfaces to test for accessibility compliance  
* Requires careful UX design to avoid confusion about which modality to use and when  
  ---

## **Accessibility Design Principles (Non-Negotiable Across All Options)**

The instructor tool should target WCAG 2.1 AAA for core instructor-facing screens, not just AA. The instructors are themselves the primary users and they are all blind or low-vision. The following criteria are especially critical for this context:

| Principle | What it means in practice |
| :---- | :---- |
| No timing requirements | No auto-expiring modals, no session timeouts mid-task |
| Single-switch navigation | Every action reachable via Tab, Enter, Space, or Escape alone |
| No info conveyed by colour alone | Status indicators must include text labels or icons, not just red/green |
| Audio description for all data output | Charts and visual summaries need a text equivalent readable by screen reader |
| High contrast support | User-togglable high-contrast theme; minimum 4.5:1 ratio for all body text |
| No hover-only UI | Tooltips and secondary info must be keyboard-accessible |
| Braille display compatibility | Avoid AJAX-heavy or dynamically updating interfaces; braille display users need stable DOM structure |

Note: many CNIB instructors and participants use both a screen reader and screen magnification software simultaneously. The tool cannot assume a purely audio or purely visual interaction model.

## **One Additional Proposal: Clarity as a Passive Data Source**

CNIB's AI chatbot Clarity was estimated to be approximately six weeks from deployment as of late March. Once live, every question a student sends to Clarity during or after a session is a signal about curriculum confusion and skill gaps. Rather than designing separate mid-lesson check-in surveys, the team should propose a Clarity query log analysis layer as part of Phase 2\. This is fully passive, adds zero burden to instructors, and will likely be richer than any multiple-choice feedback form.

## **Trainer Questions for Task 3**

1. When you prepare for a class, do you typically open any tools beforehand to check on student progress, or do you rely on memory and notes from the previous session?  
2. During class, how do you currently sense who is keeping up versus falling behind? Is it mostly intuition, or do you have any passive signals?  
3. Would using a voice command during a break feel natural, or would it feel like extra admin on top of teaching?  
4. What screen reader and OS do you use personally day-to-day? (e.g., JAWS on Windows, VoiceOver on Mac/iPhone, NVDA)  
5. Are there any tools you already use, teaching or personal, that you find genuinely easy and intuitive? What makes them work well for you?  
6. How much time do you realistically spend between sessions reviewing student progress? Is it closer to 5 minutes or an hour?  
7. When Clarity launches, will instructors be able to see what topics students are asking about, or is that data only visible on the student side?

# **Task 4: Assistive Technology Research Brief**

Owner:Aaina

Objective: Build background on assistive technology and the lived learning experience of differently abled individuals, with a focus on blind, low-vision, and deafblind participants, so every proposal the team makes is grounded in real constraints rather than assumptions.

---

## **Who the Learners Are**

The CNIB Tech Academy primarily serves differently abled individuals, the majority of whom are blind, low-vision, or deafblind. However, participants may also present with additional disabilities including motor impairments, cognitive differences, acquired brain injuries, and age-related disabilities that compound the challenge of learning new technology. Many participants are adults who experienced vision loss later in life, meaning they are adapting to assistive technology at the same time they are navigating a significant change in identity and independence. This is not a homogeneous group, and program design that treats all learners as identical will consistently underserve the most vulnerable participants.

---

## **The Assistive Technology Landscape**

The primary tools used by blind and low-vision individuals for digital tasks fall into two categories.

Screen Readers read on-screen content aloud and enable keyboard navigation:

* JAWS (Job Access With Speech): The most widely used professional screen reader. Windows only. Steep learning curve, highly customizable. Common among working-age adults and professionals.  
* NVDA (NonVisual Desktop Access): Free, open-source Windows screen reader. Popular in educational and lower-income contexts.  
* VoiceOver: Built into all Apple devices (Mac, iPhone, iPad). More beginner-friendly. Common among participants who start on iOS before moving to desktop.  
* Narrator: Windows built-in option. Less feature-rich than JAWS or NVDA; rarely a power user's primary tool.

Screen Magnification Software enlarges portions of the screen for low-vision users who retain some functional sight:

* ZoomText / Fusion: Industry-standard magnification tool. "Fusion" bundles ZoomText with JAWS for users who need both simultaneously.  
* Built-in OS zoom: Used by those with milder low-vision needs on Windows or macOS.

A critical insight from CNIB's own program data: many participants use both magnification and a screen reader at the same time to maximize speed and accuracy. This is not redundancy; it is a deliberate complementary strategy. Any tool that assumes purely audio or purely visual interaction will create friction for a large portion of the learner population.

---

## **Why AT Device Fragmentation Is a Structural Problem**

CNIB's post-course survey feedback explicitly flagged the mixing of different AT setups in a single virtual classroom as a source of confusion and frustration. Here is why it matters technically:

* Keyboard shortcuts differ entirely between JAWS and VoiceOver. A shortcut demonstrated on Windows using JAWS does nothing for a participant on Mac using VoiceOver.  
* Navigation paradigms differ: JAWS uses a "virtual cursor" and "application mode" concept that has no equivalent in VoiceOver. A new user hearing an instructor describe JAWS navigation while using VoiceOver is effectively receiving instructions in a foreign dialect.  
* Terminology differs: what JAWS calls "browse mode," VoiceOver calls "cursor tracking." These are not minor differences; they cause genuine confusion in a virtual classroom.

Implication for the project: Pre-course AT profiling (exact OS, screen reader, magnifier, prior experience level) should become mandatory intake data for every future cohort. This single change unlocks subgroup bottleneck analysis, automatic breakout room grouping by device type, and personalized pacing recommendations, without requiring any new technology beyond a structured intake form.

---

## **The Learning Curve for Differently Abled Participants**

Learning assistive technology is not like learning a new app. For most participants in this program, it is closer to learning a new language while simultaneously processing a major life change.

* Average time to functional proficiency: Approximately two years for fluent screen reader use, based on CNIB's own data. The 6-week academy is accelerating a process that would otherwise take far longer. That context matters when evaluating what "success" looks like at week 6\.  
* Cognitive load is high for everyone, and higher for those with additional disabilities:Screen reader navigation requires memorizing dozens of keyboard shortcuts and maintaining a mental model of page structure that sighted users absorb visually in seconds. For participants with cognitive differences, memory impairments, or brain injuries, this load is compounded further.  
* Transfer difficulty: Skills learned on one screen reader do not transfer directly to another. A participant who learned VoiceOver on iPhone is not ahead of a complete beginner when the academy uses JAWS on Windows. They may actually need to unlearn ingrained habits before they can progress.  
* Motor and physical disabilities add another layer: Participants with limited hand or arm mobility may rely on switch access, eye-tracking software, or foot pedals rather than standard keyboards. The current program design does not explicitly account for these cases, and the instructor tool should not either without first asking trainers whether this population is present.  
* Error recovery costs more: When a sighted, fully abled user makes a mistake, they scan the screen to understand the new state in seconds. A screen reader user must navigate linearly through content to re-orient, which takes longer and compounds frustration, especially for participants already managing anxiety around technology.  
  ---

## **The Emotional and Psychological Dimension**

This is the most underweighted factor in technology-focused program design and the most important one for the CNIB context.

Acquired disability and the identity shift: The majority of CNIB academy participants did not grow up blind or low-vision. Many experienced sudden or progressive vision loss as adults through illness, accident, diabetes, or aging. They are learning AT while grieving a loss of independence, professional identity, and the version of themselves that navigated the world with ease. This is not background context; it is the central emotional reality of the learning environment.

Confidence collapse is the dominant risk: Participants who were formerly competent and independent technology users may feel profoundly deskilled. The Leitner-scale confidence data in CNIB's survey is not a soft metric; it is likely the strongest leading indicator of whether a student completes the program and continues using AT after it ends. A student who finishes 6 weeks but exits with low confidence is a quiet failure that a binary completion flag will never capture.

Differently abled learners face compounded stigma: Participants with multiple disabilities, cognitive differences, or slower processing speeds may feel additional shame about falling behind in a class that is already designed for people with one specific disability type. The virtual format reduces some social pressure but also removes the informal peer support that happens naturally in a physical classroom.

Learned helplessness risk: If pacing is too fast or if participants fall behind without early intervention, they may disengage not because the content is too hard but because the gap feels too large to close. This is the pattern the early warning model is specifically designed to detect and interrupt.

The human connection is load-bearing: The instructor-student relationship in this program carries emotional weight well beyond its educational function. For participants who are recently disabled and socially isolated, the academy may be one of their primary sources of structured human contact. This is why the handoff from AI tools like Clarity to human instructors needs to be deliberate and clearly signaled, not just a fallback when the bot cannot answer.

---

## **Deafblind and Multi-Disability Learners**

A subset of participants may have combined vision and hearing loss, or additional physical and cognitive disabilities. For this group:

* Screen readers using audio output are not accessible. Deafblind users require refreshable braille display output (tactile devices that render one line of braille at a time).  
* Zoom-based virtual classes require real-time text relay or an intervenor to be accessible at all.  
* Information processing speed is slower because the tactile channel is narrower than audio or visual processing. Pacing that works for a low-vision participant may be twice as fast as a deafblind participant can absorb.  
* Any instructor tool with dynamic, auto-updating content will not work reliably with braille display output, which requires a stable page structure.  
* Participants with motor disabilities may not be able to use the keyboard shortcuts that the entire AT ecosystem assumes as its baseline interaction model.

It is worth confirming with trainers exactly how many participants across recent cohorts have disabilities beyond vision impairment alone, since the program design implications are significant.

---

## **Key Implications Summary**

| Research Finding | Design or Project Implication |
| :---- | :---- |
| Majority of participants are differently abled, not a single disability profile | Tool and data collection must be flexible enough to serve varied needs, not just blind users |
| Dual AT use (magnifier plus screen reader) is common | Tool must not assume purely audio or purely visual interaction |
| Device and OS fragmentation within cohorts | Mandatory pre-course AT profiling for future cohorts |
| 2-year average proficiency curve | Week 6 outcomes are relative; metrics must track trajectory, not just endpoint |
| Confidence is the leading indicator | Leitner scores should be collected weekly, not only at course end |
| Acquired disability means emotional stakes are high | Declining engagement flags in the tool must feel supportive, not like surveillance |
| Deafblind and motor-disabled learners have specific unmet needs | Confirm prevalence with trainers before assuming program design accounts for them |
| Braille display users need stable interfaces | Avoid AJAX-heavy or dynamically updating UI in the instructor tool |
| Instructors are also differently abled | The instructor tool must be AT-compatible by design, not as an afterthought |
