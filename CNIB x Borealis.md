# Ideas

**Idea A: Early Warning Model**  
*Predicting at-risk learners using weeks 1-2 data*

CNIB runs a 6-week tech academy. Trainers detect struggling learners through manual observation \-problem \- timing. The 6-week format is too compressed, not enough runway left to course correct. Learners lost confidence & momentum. 

Specific question it solves: given only the first two weeks of a learner’s data, can we predict which learners are at risk of not completing the program successfully? 

Assumptions: 

- Weekly granular data exists & can access: hw, practice & error data recorded per week & not aggregated over 6 weeks.   
- A clear outcome variable exists: need a defined measure of success vs failure for each learner. This could be a completion flag, a proficiency score or trainer assessment. Need outcome variable for supervised learning.   
- Sufficient sample size: only 3 cohorts, we probably don't have a huge sample size. Simple models \- logistic regression, decision trees. 

Limitations: 

- Prediction \!= intervention: knowing someone is at risk, does not help them. Only flags who might need more attention.   
- Cant control from unobserved individual differences \- things like personal life circumstances, learning style etc. 

Challenges: 

- Defining at risk: is someone at risk if they drop out? Complete but low score? CNIB help needed. Different definitions will output different models. 

Behavioural econ lens:   
Currently , the between-subjects model.   
It compares learner A's week 1–2 data against learner B's week 1–2 data and asks "which of these people looks more like past dropouts?" It does not track how a single learner changes over time relative to their own baseline.

The between-subjects problem**:** Imagine learner A comes in with zero computer experience and learner B is a former office worker who recently lost their sight. In week 1, learner A completes 2 out of 5 homework assignments. Learner B completes 4 out of 5\. A naive between-subjects model flags learner A as at-risk. But what if learner A is actually progressing rapidly from a very low starting point, and by week 2 they're completing 4 out of 5? Meanwhile learner B dropped from 4 to 2 — a declining trajectory that the model might miss if it only looks at absolute levels.

What a within-subject component would add**:** Instead of just looking at "did this person complete 3 out of 5 assignments in week 2" (an absolute level), you'd also look at "how did this person's completion rate *change* from week 1 to week 2" (a trajectory). In behavioural econ terms, you're capturing the *delta*, not just the *level*. Someone going from 2/5 to 4/5 is on a very different path than someone going from 4/5 to 2/5, even if they both averaged 3/5 across the two weeks.

Features:   
Add a few trajectory-based features along too. Eg: change in HW completion rate from w1 to w2. Change in practice frequency from week 1 to week 2\. 

**Idea B: Skill Bottleneck Detection**   
*Identifying where learners get stuck in the curriculum*

CNIB knows learners struggle in their 6-week academy, but they don't have a systematic understanding of *where* in the curriculum the struggles concentrate. Trainers have anecdotal observations ("people find screen reader navigation hard") but no structured evidence showing which specific skill transitions cause the most dropoff.

Specific question it solves: Which modules or skill transitions in the 6-week curriculum have the highest failure rates, and how do failures in early modules cascade into failures in later modules?

How it works**:** Map the curriculum into discrete skills (e.g., "keyboard basics" → "tab navigation" → "screen reader commands" → "email management"). Link each homework/exercise to a specific skill. Calculate per-module failure rates. Then use unsupervised clustering (K-means, hierarchical clustering) to group learners by their error profiles across modules — the algorithm finds natural groupings like "learners who do fine until document navigation" vs. "learners who struggle from week 1 onwards." Build a dependency graph showing how failure in module X predicts failure in module Y downstream.

Assumptions:

- Homework/exercises can be tagged to specific skills, not just "week 3 homework." If exercises are general-purpose covering multiple skills, the module-level analysis gets blurry.  
- Error data exists at the exercise or task level, not just "5 errors this week." Without knowing *which* exercises caused errors, we can't identify which skills are problematic.  
- The curriculum structure is stable across the 3 cohorts. If each cohort had a different syllabus, combining data is problematic.  
- CNIB trainers will collaborate on curriculum mapping. We can't build the skill map alone — we need a trainer to walk through what each exercise covers.

Limitations**:**

- Requires more granular data than Idea A. Idea A can work with weekly aggregates; Idea B needs per-exercise, per-skill data. If CNIB only tracks completion at the weekly level, this approach is significantly weakened.  
- Curriculum bias: if a module has a high failure rate, it might be because it's genuinely hard, poorly taught, or the exercises are poorly designed. The data alone can't distinguish these explanations.  
- No individual-level prediction. Unlike Idea A, this doesn't flag specific at-risk learners. It tells you *where* problems occur, not *who* is having them right now.  
- Clustering with 30–60 learners may produce unstable results — small changes in data could shift the clusters. Results are suggestive, not definitive.

Challenges:

- Curriculum mapping is labour-intensive. Getting trainers to map every exercise to a skill takes time. Callie is on holiday until April 1st.  
- Salesforce is a CRM, not a learning management system. The data might be structured around participant records, not per-exercise performance. We won't know until Gregor shares column structure.  
- Interpreting clusters requires domain expertise. The algorithm produces groups but understanding what they mean requires CNIB trainer input.

This idea is inherently a *within-subject* approach in one important way: it tracks each individual learner's performance *across* modules, not just at a single point. You're asking "how did *this person* do on skill A vs. skill B vs. skill C?" So the unit of analysis is a learner's trajectory through the curriculum, not a snapshot.

The within-subject strength: When you cluster learners by their per-module error profiles, you're capturing each learner's personal pattern of strengths and weaknesses. Learner A might score high on keyboard basics but crash on email management. Learner B might struggle consistently across everything. The clustering captures these individual shapes, not just absolute performance levels. In behavioural econ terms, you're looking at each person's *relative* performance across tasks, which naturally controls for baseline ability — a learner who is generally slow but crashes *especially* hard on one module still shows that spike, even if their absolute scores are lower everywhere.

The between-subject component: The dependency graph and bottleneck identification is between-subjects — it pools all learners together to ask "across everyone, which modules have the highest failure rates?" This is where individual differences get washed out. A module might have a 60% failure rate overall, but that could be driven by learners with no prior computer experience (for whom it's impossible) while being easy for everyone else. Without controlling for baseline, the dependency graph could be misleading.

How to address this: You could run the bottleneck analysis separately for subgroups based on intake profile — for example, "among learners with no prior computer experience, the biggest bottleneck is X" vs. "among learners transitioning from iOS VoiceOver to JAWS, the biggest bottleneck is Y." This gives CNIB differentiated insights instead of one-size-fits-all curriculum recommendations. It also controls for observable baseline differences, which is the same logic as adding covariates in a regression.

What you still can't control: Same as Idea A — unobserved heterogeneity like motivation, personal circumstances, cognitive style. The clustering might group two learners together because they have similar error profiles, but for completely different underlying reasons. One might be unmotivated, the other might have a specific learning difficulty. The data can't tell you which.

Go with Idea A as your project. Keep Idea B in your back pocket as "future work" for your final presentation

# GPT Extracted Insights

### **1\. Insights Extracted from the Current Data**

Overall, the program is highly successful. Most participants felt "Somewhat" or "Very" confident after the program, and the majority found the lectures, labs, and homework to be "Very Effective." However, the open-ended feedback reveals a few distinct challenges related to classroom management and diverse learning curves:

* **Pacing and Varying Skill Levels:** Several participants noted that mixing beginners with advanced users caused friction. One suggested *“a more delineated class breaking up into beginners and more advanced.”*  
* **Device and Screen Reader Fragmentation:** Participants are using different tools, which creates confusion in a shared space. Feedback included requests to *“Separate users based on their screen readers”* and divide by *“Apple vs. PC.”*  
* **Classroom Management Issues:** Because the instructors lack teaching backgrounds, managing a virtual room is tough. Participants noted *“Too much chatter”* and suggested that *“interruption from participant should be reduced, questions... should be sent on the chat.”*  
* **Need for Advanced Materials:** Participants want tip sheets provided *before* the Monday sessions so they can prepare, as well as cheat sheets mapped to specific programs and screen readers.  
* **Desire for Targeted Support:** Many users requested 1-on-1 support, smaller breakout rooms during labs, or weekly check-ins to troubleshoot specific problems without holding up the larger group.

### **2\. New Features & Data to Collect**

To get a better understanding of "completion" and to aid your instructors, you need to collect data *before* and *during* the course, rather than just at the end.

**Pre-Course Data (To help structure the class):**

* **Hardware & Software Profiles:** Exactly what OS (Windows, macOS, iOS) and which screen reader/magnifier (JAWS, NVDA, VoiceOver, ZoomText) the participant uses.  
* **Baseline Competency Score:** A pre-course assessment asking them to perform a basic task (e.g., send an email with an attachment) to objectively measure their starting skill level, rather than relying on self-reported confidence.

**During-Course Data (Features of Completion):**

* **Attendance & Optional Lab Participation:** Tracking not just core lectures, but who attends the optional Friday practice labs.  
* **Milestone Completion Rates:** Instead of a binary "Did you finish the 6 weeks?", track completion of intermediate milestones (e.g., Gmail module complete, Linktree complete).  
* **Intervention Requests:** How many times a student requested 1-on-1 support or flagged that they were falling behind.  
* **Resource Engagement:** Tracking if participants actually opened the pre-reading tip sheets before the Monday lectures.

### **3\. Creating a Tool to Empower Instructors**

Since your instructors are low vision/blind and lack formal teaching backgrounds, the tool you build must be **highly accessible (screen-reader friendly)** and **pedagogically supportive** to help them manage a virtual classroom.

I recommend developing an **Accessible Instructor Dashboard & Pedagogical Assistant**. This tool would integrate with your virtual meeting platform (like Zoom/Teams) and serve three main functions:

**A. Automated "Smart" Breakout Grouping**

* **How it works:** Instructors often struggle to manually sort students into breakout rooms. The tool would use the pre-course data to automatically generate breakout rooms based on AT profiles (e.g., "The JAWS Room," "The Mac/VoiceOver Room").  
* **Why it empowers them:** It removes the cognitive load of trying to teach a Windows shortcut to a Mac user while a JAWS user is asking a different question.

**B. The "Pacing & Pulse" Checker (Classroom Management)**

* **How it works:** Instead of participants interrupting verbally, they have a simple interface with accessible hotkeys (e.g., Press 1 for "I'm keeping up", Press 2 for "I'm lost", Press 3 for "I have a question"). The instructor’s dashboard reads this data and translates it into an audio cue or high-contrast visual summary (e.g., *"60% of the class is ready to move on"*).  
* **Why it empowers them:** It solves the "too much chatter" problem mentioned in the survey. Instructors can gauge the room's understanding without opening the floor to disruptive verbal interruptions.

**C. Structured Lesson Prompter (Teaching Assistant)**

* **How it works:** Because the instructors are subject-matter experts but not trained teachers, the tool provides a structured, accessible "teleprompter" for the lesson plan. It breaks the 60-minute class into timed chunks (e.g., *10 mins: Introduce concept \-\> 15 mins: Screen share demo \-\> 5 mins: Pulse check \-\> 20 mins: Guided practice*).  
* **Why it empowers them:** It acts as a built-in pedagogical guide, ensuring they don't spend 40 minutes lecturing and run out of time for practice. It can also automatically prompt them to remind students of the specific cheat sheets available for that lesson.
