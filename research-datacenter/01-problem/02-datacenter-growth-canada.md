# Canadian Data Center Market Landscape

---

## Overview

Canada's data center market is undergoing a rapid transition from steady, incremental growth to exponential expansion. Total IT capacity reached 10.3 GW as of Q2 2025, with an additional 9-10 GW in the development pipeline. Three metropolitan hubs -- Toronto, Montreal, and Calgary -- account for 93% of total IT load, creating highly concentrated demand on specific provincial grids.

## Three Dominant Hubs

### Toronto

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Facilities | 72 |
| Operators | 42 |
| Existing capacity | >370 MW (~40% of national) |
| Role | Financial services hub, enterprise colocation |

Toronto is Canada's largest data center market by a wide margin. The cluster is anchored by the financial sector's low-latency requirements (proximity to TMX and major bank headquarters) and serves as the primary Canadian point of presence for global cloud providers.

**Key trends:**
- Expansion into suburban hyperscale zones (Vaughan, Markham, Brampton) as downtown power and space constraints tighten
- Microsoft's planned Vaughan complex: expected to consume 730 million litres of water per year for cooling
- Growing tension between data center power demand and Ontario's tightening supply-demand balance
- Bill 40 (see below) introduces ministerial oversight specifically targeting this market

### Montreal

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Facilities | 54 |
| Operators | 18 |
| Existing capacity | Significant colocation + hyperscale |
| Role | Low-carbon hyperscale, AI training |

Montreal's competitive advantage is straightforward: Quebec's grid is 94-99% hydroelectric, yielding a carbon intensity of approximately 1.7 gCO2/kWh. This is among the lowest in the world and makes Montreal the default choice for operators with public carbon reduction commitments.

**Key trends:**
- Hyperscale operators (Google, Microsoft, AWS) expanding Quebec presence specifically for sustainability reporting
- QScale's Q01 facility in Levis (~100 MW) redirects waste heat to household heating -- a model for circular energy use
- Hydro-Quebec has historically offered competitive industrial electricity rates, though new large-load contracts face increasing scrutiny
- Cold climate enables extensive free-air cooling, reducing both PUE and water consumption

### Calgary / Alberta

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Facilities | Growing rapidly |
| Upcoming capacity | ~2 GW (>25% of national pipeline) |
| Role | Energy sector data, emerging hyperscale |
| Grid carbon intensity | ~490 gCO2/kWh |

Alberta is the fastest-growing data center market in Canada, driven by energy sector data processing needs, competitive land costs, and a deregulated electricity market that facilitates large-load connections.

**Key trends:**
- More than 25% of Canada's upcoming data center capacity is planned for Alberta
- Grid remains heavily fossil-dependent (~490 gCO2/kWh), meaning each new MW of data center load carries a substantial carbon footprint
- Deregulated market structure means data center operators can negotiate power purchase agreements (PPAs) directly with generators
- Emerging renewable energy PPAs (wind + solar) could partially offset grid carbon intensity, but intermittency requires backup
- Proximity to US fiber routes and relatively low natural disaster risk

## CER Market Snapshot (October 2024)

The Canada Energy Regulator published a Market Snapshot in October 2024 that mapped 239 data centers across Canada by province. Key observations:

- Ontario and Quebec together host the majority of facilities
- Alberta's share is growing fastest in percentage terms
- The report noted that data center electricity demand is becoming a material factor in provincial energy planning
- Provincial variation in grid carbon intensity was highlighted as a key variable in national emissions accounting

This was the first time a federal energy regulator formally addressed data center siting as an energy policy issue in Canada.

## Ontario Bill 40

**Full title:** Protect Ontario by Securing Affordable Energy for Generations Act, 2025

**Royal Assent:** December 11, 2025

### Key Provisions

- Requires "covered data centre projects" to receive approval from the **Minister of Energy and Mines** before connecting to the provincial electricity grid
- The Minister may prioritize projects based on:
  - Economic benefits to Ontario
  - Strategic and security considerations
  - Local community benefits
  - Impact on electricity system reliability and affordability
- **Does not apply** to connection requests submitted before June 3, 2025
- Establishes a precedent for treating data centers as a distinct category of electricity consumer requiring special regulatory treatment

### Significance

Bill 40 is the **first Canadian legislation explicitly regulating data center grid connections**. It signals a fundamental shift from treating data centers as ordinary commercial loads to recognizing them as infrastructure-scale consumers whose siting decisions have province-wide implications.

**Implications for this project:**
- Demonstrates that provincial governments are actively seeking frameworks to evaluate data center proposals
- The evaluation criteria (economic, strategic, community, reliability) align directly with the multi-dimensional assessment this project proposes
- Creates a potential end-user for a Societal Impact Score tool: Ontario's Ministry of Energy and Mines

### Legal Analysis

Multiple Canadian law firms have published analyses of Bill 40, noting:
- The breadth of ministerial discretion in approving or denying connections
- Uncertainty about how "covered data centre projects" will be defined in regulation
- Potential for other provinces to adopt similar legislation
- Questions about whether the law could discourage investment or simply redirect it to other provinces

## Market Trajectory

The Canadian data center market has shifted from steady, predictable growth to an exponential development phase in approximately one year (2024-2025). Key inflection factors:

1. **AI demand surge:** Generative AI training and inference requirements drove hyperscale operators to seek new capacity globally, including in Canada
2. **US grid constraints:** Interconnection queue delays in key US markets (Northern Virginia, Dallas, Phoenix) pushed operators to explore Canadian alternatives
3. **ESG commitments:** Major cloud providers' net-zero pledges made Quebec and British Columbia's low-carbon grids strategically attractive
4. **Sovereign data requirements:** Canadian data residency regulations for financial services and government workloads ensure a baseline of domestic demand
